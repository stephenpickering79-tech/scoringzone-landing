Variant 0 · Handicap

What a short game handicap actually tells you

Your full handicap index tells you how you score. It doesn’t tell you which part of your game is costing the shots — and for most golfers breaking 90, the answer is inside 50 yards.

A short game handicap benchmarks the same shots every time, so the number moves only when your short game genuinely does.

How to bring it down

Pick one distance, score yourself against a benchmark, and repeat it weekly. Progress you can see is progress you keep doing.

Variant 1 · Putting

Why your putts miss left

An open or closed face at impact almost always comes from the hands and wrists taking over — a little flick, a little steer, a squeeze of grip pressure at the last second. The putter face reacts instantly to any of it.

Quiet the hands and the face stays square. That’s why the fixes below start with your setup and grip, not your stroke.

Aim the face, then the body

Set the putter face behind the ball and aim it at your target first — most golfers aim their feet and shoulders and let the face fall wherever.

Variant 2 · Chipping

The chipping motion

The chipping motion is a pendulum from the shoulders, not a swing from the wrists. Your hands stay quiet, the arms and shoulders swing as one piece, and the ball gets in the way of a downward-moving club face.

If you can stroke a 30-foot putt with a putter, you can chip a golf ball with a 9-iron. The motion is almost identical — the only difference is loft on the club face.

The setup — 5 checkpoints

Feet about 6–8 inches apart. A narrow stance keeps the swing compact and stops you making a full body turn.

Variant 3 · Stats / handicap

What strokes gained actually measures

If a tour pro averages 70 and you shoot 90, your total strokes gained against the tour benchmark is −20. The interesting question isn’t that you’re 20 strokes worse — it’s where those 20 strokes are going.

Strokes gained breaks the answer down to the shot level, which is what makes it more useful than putts per round or greens in regulation.

Where the numbers come from

Strokes gained didn’t exist as a usable metric until 2011, when Columbia Business School professor Mark Broadie published the foundational research.
