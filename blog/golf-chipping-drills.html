Golf Chipping Drills: 6 That Actually Lower Scores

Six Scored Drills to Sharpen Contact, Distance Control, and Up-and-Downs

July 14, 2026 · 8 min read · Stephen Pickering

Key takeaway: Random chipping to one flag doesn’t transfer because there’s no target and no score. These six drills each have a setup, a target, and a way to keep score — the Ten Yarder and Landing Spot Ladder build clean contact and landing control, Ladder Up and Par 2 build distance and up-and-down conversion, and 21 Points and the 15-Minute Blitz add pressure. Pick one foundation drill and one pressure drill per session, keep the scores, and watch your up-and-down percentage climb.

Most golfers chip the same way they practise everything else — rake a pile of balls to one flag, hit until it feels okay, and go home. Then they wonder why the up-and-down never comes when it counts. The problem isn’t effort. It’s that there’s no target and no score, so nothing sharpens.

These six golf chipping drills fix that. Each one has a specific setup, a clear target, and a way to keep score, so you finish every session knowing whether you got better or just got tired. Work through them in order — foundation first, pressure last — and your greenside game stops being a guess.

Why Random Chipping Doesn’t Transfer

No score means no feedback

Chipping to a flag with ten balls tells you almost nothing. You remember the two that finished stone dead and forget the six that scuttled fifteen feet past. Without a number, your brain edits the session into whatever story it likes. A drill with a score forces you to face the actual result — and that’s where improvement starts.

Contact and roll-out are two different skills

A good chip needs clean, ball-first contact and the right landing spot so the ball rolls out to the hole. Most golfers only ever practise the strike and completely ignore where the ball lands. The drills below train both — separately, then together — so you’re not fixing one while breaking the other.

6 Golf Chipping Drills That Build a Real Short Game

1The Ten Yarder

Chip ten balls from just off the green to a hole ten yards away, using whichever club you’re most comfortable with. Count how many finish inside three feet. That number is your baseline — write it down.

This is the foundation drill because ten yards is the most common greenside distance you’ll face, and finishing inside three feet is what turns a likely bogey into a tap-in par. Target: 6 out of 10 to start, 8 out of 10 once you’re sharp.

2The Landing Spot Ladder

Lay a towel on the green as a landing target about a third of the way to the hole. Chip five balls and try to land each one on the towel — ignore where it finishes, watch only where it lands. Then move the towel closer or further and repeat.

Chipping is landing-spot control, not hole control. Once you can land the ball on a dinner-plate-sized target on demand, distance takes care of itself because you’re controlling the one variable that matters. Score: count clean landings out of five.

3Ladder Up

Place five clubs or alignment sticks on the green, the first at 10 feet and each one 2 feet further apart. Chip four balls to land between each pair of gates, working closest to furthest — then chip four balls back, furthest to closest. Miss a gate and you start the whole ladder again. Record how many attempts it takes to complete it.

This drill trains distance control under a light bit of pressure — the further you get, the more you don’t want to miss and restart. It’s the single best drill for golfers who leave chips short or blow them long.

4Par 2

Set up nine different chip shots around the green — different lies, distances, and angles. Play each one as a par 2: one chip, one putt, done. Even if the chip finishes close, you must putt from at least a putter’s length. No gimmies. Count how many holes you complete in two or less.

Par 2 is the drill that mirrors the course, because you never get the same chip twice out there. It also stitches your chipping and putting together, which is what an up-and-down actually is.

521 Points

Chip to a hole and score each ball: 5 points for holing out, 3 for inside one foot, 1 for inside three feet. Keep chipping until you reach exactly 21 — go over and you bust, and start again. Record how many chips it took.

This is where the pressure ramps up. As you close in on 21, every chip carries weight, and you learn to deliver a good shot when the number matters. That’s the skill that shows up on the course.

6The 15-Minute Blitz

Pick one club, mark three zones around the hole at 5, 10, and 15 feet, and give yourself fifteen minutes to score as high as you can against the clock. One club, moving targets, a timer running — it’s the closest a practice session gets to the feeling of standing over a chip you can’t afford to waste.

Use this one as your assessment. Run it at the start of a practice block and again a few weeks later. If the score’s gone up, your chipping’s improved. Turn These Drills Into Lower Scores

Six drills is more than enough — the mistake is doing all of them badly instead of two of them properly. Pick a foundation drill and a pressure drill each session. Keep the scores. The moment you can see the numbers trending, practice stops feeling like a chore and starts feeling like a game you want to win. Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I practise chipping drills?

Three short sessions a week beats one long one. Twenty focused minutes of scored chipping drills — where you’re counting results, not just raking balls — will move your up-and-down percentage faster than an hour of aimless chipping once a fortnight.

What is the best chipping drill for beginners?

Start with the Ten Yarder: chip ten balls to a hole ten yards away and count how many finish inside three feet. It builds the two things beginners need most — clean contact and a feel for how far the ball rolls out.

Can I practise chipping drills at home?

Yes. A towel or a coin makes a landing target on any patch of lawn or carpet, and the landing-spot drills transfer directly. You won’t get roll-out feedback indoors, but you can groove clean contact and a repeatable landing point anywhere.

What is the fastest way to improve chipping?

Score every session and track it. Once you know your baseline — how many chips out of ten you get inside three feet — you can see whether you’re actually improving instead of guessing. Measured practice is the fastest route to a lower short-game handicap.
